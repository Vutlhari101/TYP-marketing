About the project

One system, four jobs to do.

A professional services firm runs on two things happening well: new people becoming productive quickly, and client work landing on the date it was promised. Most firms track these in different places, by different people, and discover the gap between them too late.

SyncCore puts both in one system. A project manager commits to a date and breaks the work into tasks. A team manager splits those tasks into deliverables and assigns them to people. Those people do the work, submit it for review, and nobody approves their own. HR watches a new joiner's first ninety days against the same clock.

Every figure on every screen is derived from that one chain of accountability. Nothing is typed in twice, and nothing is self-reported.

  • Work, not status

    A task's state is derived from its deliverables, and a project's from its tasks. Nobody updates a progress bar by hand.

  • Separation of duties

    A person cannot approve their own work. That single rule is what makes the quality figures mean anything.

  • Decisions, not dashboards

    Each number answers a question somebody actually has, and opens the list of work behind it.

The challenge

What goes wrong without it

01

New joiners drift

A graduate completes training with no idea whether they are on pace. Their mentor finds out weeks later, usually by accident.

SyncCore: every onboarding step carries its own deadline, worked back from the start date. Mentor and joiner read the identical screen.

02

Late is discovered late

A project reports "on track" until the week it misses. Percentage complete tells you nothing without the time already spent.

SyncCore: time elapsed is shown against work finished, and the delivery pace of the last four weeks forecasts the landing date.

03

The wrong person is blamed

Overdue work gets counted against whoever holds it — including work finished weeks ago that is still waiting on a review.

SyncCore: review latency is measured as its own figure, so a bottleneck is attributed to the person who owns the decision.

The platform

Built around the work itself

Delivery intelligence

Numbers that name the next action

Throughput, cycle time, on-time rate, first-pass approval, rework, ageing work in progress and review latency — seven measures defined once and reused for every role. Medians rather than averages, so one abandoned task cannot distort a figure, and a rate with no denominator shows a dash rather than a misleading zero.

  • Every tile opens the list of work behind it
  • Compared against your own past pace, never a league table
  • Exports to real Excel and PDF, generated in the browser

Employee onboarding

A first ninety days you can see

A structured checklist of training, quizzes and meetings, each with a deadline derived from the joining date. Quiz attempts are kept in full, not just the pass — needing three goes at an ethics assessment says something the pass mark hides, and it routes the person to their mentor before it becomes a problem.

  • Identical view for the joiner and their mentor
  • One click from a concerning figure to booking a meeting
  • HR sees where people stall across the whole intake

Project delivery

From client promise to finished file

Projects carry a real start and end date. Tasks go to teams; deliverables go to people. Briefs travel attached to the work, and completed files return to the same place, so in six months the answer to "what was actually delivered" is one click away.

  • Status rolls up automatically — nobody types a status
  • Kanban, list, table and Gantt views of the same work
  • Comments with @mentions against the deliverable itself

Role-based design

Four people, four versions of the truth

The same data, scoped and framed for the decision each role actually makes. Select a role to see what they get.

“Am I doing well, and what do I raise?”

A new joiner sees their ninety-day timeline, step completion against the day they are on, and every quiz attempt. An established employee sees their own delivery record: on-time rate, usual time to finish, what is open and what is genuinely overdue as opposed to sitting in someone else's review queue.

The decision it drives: what to raise in a one-to-one, and when to ask a mentor for help.

  • Steps doneAgainst the day you are on
  • Quiz attemptsRetakes as an early warning
  • On-time rateOnly work with a date to judge
  • Open and overdueWith where each item is stuck

“Who needs support, and am I the bottleneck?”

Load across every team member, work that has started but stopped moving, rework hotspots framed as a coaching signal rather than a blame list, and the deadline cliff for the next fortnight. Including the figure managers never measure: how long work waits on their own review.

The decision it drives: who to rebalance, who needs help, and what to approve first.

  • Load by personOpen against overdue, side by side
  • Stalled workStarted, untouched, still rescuable
  • Rework hotspotsSent back most often
  • Review latencyTime waiting on the manager

“Will this land, and do I need more people?”

Projects past their deadline first, because that is the only thing that cannot wait. Then time elapsed against work finished for every project with real dates, a forecast landing date from the pace of the last four weeks, and team contribution scored on both volume and how cleanly the work landed.

The decision it drives: which client conversation to have this morning, and where to place the next piece of work.

  • Projects at riskPast due, stalled, or forecast late
  • Time used vs work doneTwo facts, not an opinion
  • Sent back for changesA signal about the brief
  • Team rankingHalf volume, half quality

“Is onboarding working, and where does it fail?”

The onboarding funnel across the whole intake with the median days to completion, and crucially the step people stall at — a step with a queue behind it is a content problem, not a people problem. Plus quiz pass rates, mentorship coverage, and enquiry response times by department.

The decision it drives: what to fix in the programme, and which capability gap to hire against.

  • Where onboarding stallsThe first incomplete step per person
  • Attempts per passLow pass rates are content problems
  • Mentorship coverageMeetings held against proposed
  • Quality by tenureDoes the programme actually work

Technology

How it is built

Three clients over one API and one database, containerised so the whole system starts with a single command.

Web client

  • React 18
  • Vite
  • Zustand
  • Recharts
  • Tailwind CSS
  • React Router

API

  • Node.js 20
  • Express 5
  • JWT and bcrypt
  • Multer uploads
  • Nodemailer
  • Raw parameterised SQL

Database

  • PostgreSQL 16
  • Versioned migrations
  • Window functions
  • Percentile aggregates
  • JSONB activity log
  • Schema-level constraints

Mobile

  • React Native 0.74
  • TypeScript
  • React Navigation
  • AsyncStorage
  • Axios
  • Android build

Deployment

  • Docker Compose
  • Multi-stage builds
  • nginx reverse proxy
  • Named volumes
  • Health-gated startup
  • Environment secrets

Reporting

  • SheetJS for Excel
  • jsPDF for documents
  • AutoTable layout
  • Client-side generation
  • Typeset, not screenshots
  • No server round trip

This marketing site is written in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no frameworks, no build step and no external dependencies.
